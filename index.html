The news

Why I wrote Neuro Games

My new book, Neuro Games, is now on Amazon.

Many of you know me as the director of Coddy. For more than 10 years I have worked with children, and again and again I saw the same thing: kids engage differently when they can think and move at the same time.

At home, while practicing with my own kids, I collected activities from textbooks and video lectures. Little by little the idea grew to bring them together in one book.

My toughest examiner was our grandmother, a doctor with 40 years of practice who spent years caring for children at the Suvorov Military School and a children's wushu federation. She quizzed me on anatomy and physiology before my exams, and her checks would make Minos and Procrustes look lenient.

The result is 60 short challenges, most of them pairing a puzzle with a move. One minute your child is spotting what changed in a picture, the next they are reaching a hand to the opposite knee or standing on one foot.

The illustrations were drawn by AI from my detailed instructions, which helped keep the book affordable. The concept, the choice and adaptation of every activity, the two age versions, and all the checking and redoing are human work.

— Natalia Milos (I write as Milos, short for my surname, Miloserdnaya)

What's inside

Look, think, move

Eight levels, from Look + Focus and Left + Right to Logic + Hands and Super Brain. Every activity has two parts, Just Play for ages 6–7 and Level Up! for ages 8–10, so siblings can share one book.

Look closely

Study a picture, then find the five things that changed in the second one.

Cross your body

Follow picture commands where one arm crosses the middle of the body: lazy eights, skating steps, a hand reaching to the other side.

Plan the path

Find the way from START to GOAL and add one block to your tower for every step.

One hand thinks, the other moves

Finish a pattern with one hand while the other moves beads from bowl to bowl.

Math on the move

Shopping sums while marching in place or balancing on one leg.

Answers and a certificate

Answer keys for every puzzle with a right answer, and a certificate to celebrate the finish.

What research says

Why scientists keep looking at movement and thinking together

Researchers have been studying how physical activity relates to children's attention and thinking skills for years, and a growing line of work looks at movement that also makes kids think. Here is what some of the key studies found, in plain words.

Active after-school program, 9 months

Children aged 7–9 in a daily, roughly two-hour after-school physical activity program improved more than a comparison group on tasks of attention control and mental flexibility. Those who attended more gained more.

Hillman et al., Pediatrics, 2014 →

US Physical Activity Guidelines review

The expert committee behind the 2018 US guidelines found moderate evidence that physical activity is linked to better thinking skills in children aged 6–13, and strong evidence that a single bout of moderate-to-vigorous activity improves thinking for a short time afterwards.

Erickson et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2019 →

Movement that makes kids think

In a 6-week PE study with children aged 10–12, games that were physically active and mentally demanding improved one of the skills measured: switching between rules. Plain aerobic exercise with the same fitness gain did not.

Schmidt et al., Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology, 2015 →

What helps executive functions

A large review of programs for children's planning and self-control skills concluded that "mindless" aerobic exercise does little, while activities that combine movement with thinking look more promising, though gains mostly show up in the skills practiced.

Diamond & Ling, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 2016 →

Short movement breaks in class

Across dozens of primary-school studies, classroom activity breaks and active lessons were linked to children staying more on-task.

Watson et al., International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity, 2017 →

Activity, learning and grades

A systematic review found generally positive links between physical activity and children's thinking, and no sign that time spent moving harms learning, though the authors call the evidence inconsistent.

Donnelly et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2016 →

Why it matters now

Kids need daily movementThe World Health Organization recommends that children aged 5–17 get an average of 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day and limit recreational screen time.
WHO guidelines, 2020 →
Most teens don't move enoughAbout 4 in 5 school-going 11–17-year-olds worldwide did not meet that 60-minute goal (2016 data).
Guthold et al., Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 2020 →
Ten minutes fitsShort, playful breaks are easy to add after school or on a rainy weekend: a page, a puzzle, a move.

Neuro Games is built on these ideas. The book itself has not been tested in a scientific study, and it is an activity book, not a treatment.
