Look. Think. Move.

Eight levels, from Look + Focus and Left + Right to Logic + Hands and Super Brain. Every activity has two parts: Just Play for ages 6–7 and Level Up! for ages 8–10, so siblings can share one book.

About the author

Natalia Milos

“I made this book for my own children. They were its first testers.”

Director of Coddy, a coding school for kids in New York. For more than 10 years she has worked with children and kept seeing the same thing: kids engage differently when they can think and move at the same time. Neuro Games collects the activities she practiced at home with her own kids.

Her toughest examiner was the family's grandmother, a doctor with 40 years of practice who spent years caring for children at the Suvorov Military School and a children's wushu federation. She quizzed Natalia on anatomy and physiology before her exams, with checks that would make Minos and Procrustes look lenient. The illustrations were drawn by AI from Natalia's detailed instructions, which kept the book affordable. The concept, every activity, both age versions and all the checking are her work.

What research says

Why moving and thinking belong together.

Researchers have studied how physical activity relates to children's attention and thinking for years. Here is what some of the key studies found, in plain words.

Kids need daily movementWHO recommends an average of 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day for ages 5–17, and limiting recreational screen time.WHO guidelines, 2020
Most teens don't move enoughAbout 4 in 5 school-going 11–17-year-olds worldwide did not meet the 60-minute goal (2016 data).Guthold et al., Lancet CAH, 2020
US activity guidelines reviewModerate evidence that activity is linked to better thinking in kids 6–13, and strong evidence that a single bout of moderate-to-vigorous activity helps thinking for a short time afterwards.Erickson et al., MSSE, 2019
5 more studies+
Movement breaks in classAcross dozens of primary-school studies, activity breaks were linked to kids staying more on-task.Watson et al., IJBNPA, 2017
Activity, learning and gradesA systematic review found generally positive links between activity and kids' thinking, and no sign that moving harms learning. The authors call the evidence inconsistent.Donnelly et al., MSSE, 2016
What helps executive functions“Mindless” aerobic exercise does little. Activities that combine movement with thinking look more promising, though gains mostly show up in the skills practiced.Diamond & Ling, DCN, 2016
Movement that makes kids thinkIn a 6-week PE study with kids aged 10–12, games that were active and mentally demanding improved one of the skills measured: switching between rules. Plain aerobic exercise did not.Schmidt et al., JSEP, 2015
After-school activity, 9 monthsKids aged 7–9 in a daily, roughly two-hour activity program improved more than a comparison group on attention control and mental flexibility. More attendance, bigger gains.Hillman et al., Pediatrics, 2014

Neuro Games is built on these ideas. The book itself has not been tested in a scientific study, and it is an activity book, not a treatment.
